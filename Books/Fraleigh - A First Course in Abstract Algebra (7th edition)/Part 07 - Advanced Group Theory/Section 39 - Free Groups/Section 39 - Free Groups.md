
# 39 Free Groups
In this section and Section 40 we discuss a portion of group theory that is of great interest not only in algebra but in topology as well. In fact, an excellent and readable discussion of free groups and presentations of groups is found in Crowell and Fox [46, Chapters 3 and 4].

## Words and Reduced Words

> [!definition|f5602031-cdba-543f-8a92-0b9ce6f6f9e5.library at=738b4b95-fb44-51fb-9b2e-d43d5418ccaa] Alphabet, Syllable, and Word in Group Theory
> Let $A$ be any (not necessarily finite) set of elements $a_{i}$ for $i \in I$. We think of $A$ as an alphabet and of the $a_{i}$ as letters in the alphabet. Any symbol of the form $a_{i}{ }^{n}$ with $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ is a syllable and a finite string $w$ of syllables written in juxtaposition is a word. We also introduce the empty word 1, which has no syllables.

> [!example|a45ef280-e1e2-5c42-9536-2c4b4786e384.local at=43d4ce4f-34f3-5e18-9ebc-4444c0ed8232] Example 39.1
> Give examples of words over $A=\{a_1,a_2,a_3\}$.

##### solution

Let $A=\left\{a_{1}, a_{2}, a_{3}\right\}$. Then

$$
a_{1} a_{3}^{-4} a_{2}^{2} a_{3}, \quad a_{2}^{3} a_{2}^{-1} a_{3} a_{1}^{2} a_{1}^{-7}, \quad \text { and } \quad a_{3}^{2}
$$

are all words, if we follow the convention of understanding that $a_{i}{ }^{1}$ is the same as $a_{i}$. $\square$

> [!definition|b598ec61-43b5-5d44-a5e8-8e558e8f1ce6.library at=1884fe3f-b207-54f2-b148-de9fb87d5448] Elementary Contractions and Reduced Words
> Let $A=\{a_i\mid i\in I\}$ be an alphabet. There are two natural types of modifications of certain words, the elementary contractions. The first type consists of replacing an occurrence of $a_{i}{ }^{m} a_{i}{ }^{n}$ in a word by $a_{i}{ }^{m+n}$. The second type consists of replacing an occurrence of $a_{i}{ }^{0}$ in a word by 1 , that is, dropping it out of the word. By means of a finite number of elementary contractions, every word can be changed to a reduced word, one for which no more elementary contractions are possible. Note that these elementary contractions formally amount to the usual manipulations of integer exponents.

> [!example|ad30ce02-8c96-5d3a-9215-cb2062d1ece6.local at=06484d9f-c323-5aab-a670-00106e943a89] Example 39.2
> Find the reduced form of $a_2^3a_2^{-1}a_3a_1^2a_1^{-7}$.

##### solution

The reduced form of the word $a_{2}{ }^{3} a_{2}{ }^{-1} a_{3} a_{1}{ }^{2} a_{1}{ }^{-7}$ of Example 39.1 is $a_{2}{ }^{2} a_{3} a_{1}{ }^{-5}$. $\square$

It should be said here once and for all that we are going to gloss over several points that some books spend pages proving, usually by complicated induction arguments broken down into many cases. For example, suppose we are given a word and wish to find its reduced form. There may be a variety of elementary contractions that could be performed first. How do we know that the reduced word we end up with is the same no matter in what order we perform the elementary contractions? The student will probably say this is obvious. Some authors spend considerable effort proving this. The author tends to agree here with the student. Proofs of this sort he regards as tedious, and they have never made him more comfortable about the situation. However, the author is the first to acknowledge that he is not a great mathematician. In deference to the fact that many mathematicians feel that these things do need considerable discussion, we shall mark an occasion when we just state such facts by the phrase, "It would seem obvious that," keeping the quotation marks.

## Free Groups

Let the set of all reduced words formed from our alphabet $A$ be $F[A]$. We now make $F[A]$ into a group in a natural way. For $w_{1}$ and $w_{2}$ in $F[A]$, define $w_{1} \cdot w_{2}$ to be the reduced form of the word obtained by the juxtaposition $w_{1} w_{2}$ of the two words.

> [!example|fe0bbf50-13cb-5544-b319-0e98637d7f18.local at=55bc5c53-75f8-59c9-9efe-35dbcf0081bf] Example 39.3
> Compute $w_1\cdot w_2$ for $w_1=a_2^3a_1^{-5}a_3^2$ and $w_2=a_3^{-2}a_1^2a_3a_2^{-2}$.

##### solution

If

$$
w_{1}=a_{2}^{3} a_{1}^{-5} a_{3}^{2}
$$

and

$$
w_{2}=a_{3}^{-2} a_{1}^{2} a_{3} a_{2}^{-2},
$$

then $w_{1} \cdot w_{2}=a_{2}{ }^{3} a_{1}{ }^{-3} a_{3} a_{2}{ }^{-2}$. $\square$
"It would seem obvious that" this operation of multiplication on $F[A]$ is well defined and associative. The empty word 1 acts as an identity element. "It would seem obvious that" given a reduced word $w \in F[A]$, if we form the word obtained by first writing the syllables of $w$ in the opposite order and second by replacing each $a_{i}^{n}$ by $a_{i}^{-n}$, then the resulting word $w^{-1}$ is a reduced word also, and

$$
w \cdot w^{-1}=w^{-1} \cdot w=1 .
$$

Definition 39.4

> [!definition|cf9ae826-8015-55f2-8e16-41ed9f55eef8.library at=016b2056-0247-5728-abd9-d4d154cc7c99] Free Group Generated by an Alphabet
> Let the set of all reduced words formed from an alphabet $A$ be $F[A]$. For $w_1$ and $w_2$ in $F[A]$, define $w_1\cdot w_2$ to be the reduced form of the word obtained by the juxtaposition $w_1w_2$ of the two words.
>
> The group $F[A]$ is the free group generated by $A$.

Look back at Theorem 7.6 and the definition preceding it to see that the present use of the term generated is consistent with the earlier use.

Starting with a group $G$ and a generating set $\left\{a_{i} \mid i \in I\right\}$ which we will abbreviate by $\left\{a_{i}\right\}$, we might ask if $G$ is free on $\left\{a_{i}\right\}$, that is, if $G$ is essentially the free group generated by $\left\{a_{i}\right\}$. We define precisely what this is to mean.
Definition 39.5

> [!definition|32570f1a-68bc-5659-b0a7-5c010083559f.library at=49d83b7c-3f87-5cb4-bfd9-4c8bf16b8d81] Free Generators and Free Groups
> If $G$ is a group with a set $A=\left\{a_{i}\right\}$ of generators, and if $G$ is isomorphic to $F[A]$ under a map $\phi: G \rightarrow F[A]$ such that $\phi\left(a_{i}\right)=a_{i}$, then $G$ is free on $A$, and the $a_{i}$ are free generators of $G$. A group is free if it is free on some nonempty set $A$. $\square$
>
> Here $F[A]$ is the free group generated by $A$.

> [!example|c576d334-d5d5-5f12-8e67-5dd22361c585.local at=f0d4de83-f84c-519e-b5bd-f098fb727783] Example 39.6
> Give an example of a free group on one generator.

##### solution

The only example of a free group that has occurred before is $\mathbb{Z}$, which is free on one generator. Note that every free group is infinite. $\square$

Refer to the literature for proofs of the next three theorems. We will not be using these results. They are stated simply to inform us of these interesting facts.
Theorem 39.7

> [!theorem|36b3358b-325d-5990-9154-c080f5211238.library at=b78cc1cd-73e0-50ff-9bf4-6dea56f8c460] Invariance of the Number of Free Generators
> If a group $G$ is free on $A$ and also on $B$, then the sets $A$ and $B$ have the same number of elements; that is, any two sets of free generators of a free group have the same cardinality.
Definition 39.8

> [!definition|2c216af9-e968-5749-b5ed-a8d9c696a3ca.library at=a1a7394d-6521-50fa-846e-ae43bd5ea386] Rank of a Free Group
> If $G$ is free on $A$, the number of elements in $A$ is the rank of the free group $G$. $\square$

Actually, the next theorem is quite evident from Theorem 39.7.
Theorem 39.9

> [!theorem|49c42a29-c902-5cd7-91fc-4712dd5a4d45.library at=d6cf2c27-43e5-560f-a325-6e2a9e4ab653] Classification of Free Groups by Rank
> Two free groups are isomorphic if and only if they have the same rank.
Theorem 39.10

> [!theorem|f1915a6d-54a6-5dc4-a51c-3dc4394d1aa0.library at=a5bc09fe-74f5-575f-82ff-6e45ae68caf5] Nontrivial Subgroups of Free Groups Are Free
> A nontrivial proper subgroup of a free group is free.

> [!example|009328ed-e1cb-5328-b070-ba67295cd4cb.local at=a9c9ef35-e11e-5828-8684-dcaf85dfe553] Example 39.11
> In $F[\{x,y\}]$, consider $y_k=x^kyx^{-k}$ for $k\geq0$. Compare the rank of the subgroup generated by the $y_k$ with the rank of the whole group.

##### solution

Let $F[\{x, y\}]$ be the free group on $\{x, y\}$. Let

$$
y_{k}=x^{k} y x^{-k}
$$

for $k \geq 0$. The $y_{k}$ for $k \geq 0$ are free generators for the subgroup of $F[\{x, y\}]$ that they generate. This illustrates that although a subgroup of a free group is free, the rank of the subgroup may be much greater than the rank of the whole group! $\square$

## Homomorphisms of Free Groups

Our work in this section will be concerned primarily with homomorphisms defined on a free group. The results here are simple and elegant.
Theorem 39.12

> [!theorem|c686e70d-d9f0-53fc-8ea3-2bfb037973ce.library at=dd64abb3-e3f1-5b59-960f-cca11f06d9f2] Homomorphisms Determined by Generators
> Let $G$ be generated by $A=\left\{a_{i} \mid i \in I\right\}$ and let $G^{\prime}$ be any group. If $a_{i}{ }^{\prime}$ for $i \in I$ are any elements in $G^{\prime}$, not necessarily distinct, then there is at most one homomorphism $\phi: G \rightarrow G^{\prime}$ such that $\phi\left(a_{i}\right)=a_{i}{ }^{\prime}$. If $G$ is free on $A$, then there is exactly one such homomorphism.

##### proof

Let $\phi$ be a homomorphism from $G$ into $G^{\prime}$ such that $\phi\left(a_{i}\right)=a_{i}{ }^{\prime}$. Now by Theorem 7.6, for any $x \in G$ we have

$$
x=\prod_{j} a_{i_{j}}^{n_{j}}
$$

for some finite product of the generators $a_{i}$, where the $a_{i_{j}}$ appearing in the product need not be distinct. Then since $\phi$ is a homomorphism, we must have

$$
\phi(x)=\prod_{j} \phi\left(a_{i_{j}}^{n_{j}}\right)=\prod_{j}\left(a_{i_j}{ }^{\prime}\right)^{n_{j}} .
$$

Thus a homomorphism is completely determined by its values on elements of a generating set. This shows that there is at most one homomorphism such that $\phi\left(a_{i}\right)=a_{i}{ }^{\prime}$.

Now suppose $G$ is free on $A$; that is, $G=F[A]$. For

$$
x=\prod_{j} a_{i_{j}}^{n_{j}}
$$

in $G$, define $\psi: G \rightarrow G^{\prime}$ by

$$
\psi(x)=\prod_{j}\left(a_{i_{j}}^{\prime}\right)^{n_{j}} .
$$

The map is well defined, since $F[A]$ consists precisely of reduced words; no two different formal products in $F[A]$ are equal. Since the rules for computation involving exponents in $G^{\prime}$ are formally the same as those involving exponents in $G$, it is clear that $\psi(xy)=\psi(x)\psi(y)$ for any elements $x$ and $y$ in $G$, so $\psi$ is indeed a homomorphism. $\square$

Perhaps we should have proved the first part of this theorem earlier, rather than having relegated it to the exercises. Note that the theorem states that a homomorphism of a group is completely determined if we know its value on each element of a generating set. This was Exercise 46 of Section 13. In particular, a homomorphism of a cyclic group is completely determined by its value on any single generator of the group.
Theorem 39.13

> [!theorem|524ac8ed-46aa-5d18-b865-96792a073499.library at=13ac2f2f-57aa-5bff-acae-632ea368872f] Every Group Is a Homomorphic Image of a Free Group
> Every group $G^{\prime}$ is a homomorphic image of a free group $G$.

##### proof

Let $G^{\prime}=\left\{a_{i}{ }^{\prime} \mid i \in I\right\}$, and let $A=\left\{a_{i} \mid i \in I\right\}$ be a set with the same number of elements as $G^{\prime}$. Let $G=F[A]$. Then by Theorem 39.12 there exists a homomorphism $\psi$ mapping $G$ into $G^{\prime}$ such that $\psi\left(a_{i}\right)=a_{i}{ }^{\prime}$. Clearly the image of $G$ under $\psi$ is all of $G^{\prime}$. $\square$

## Another Look at Free Abelian Groups

It is important that we do not confuse the notion of a free group with the notion of a free abelian group. A free group on more than one generator is not abelian. In the preceding section, we defined a free abelian group as an abelian group that has a basis, that is, a generating set satisfying properties described in Theorem 38.1. There is another approach, via free groups, to free abelian groups. We now describe this approach.

Let $F[A]$ be the free group on the generating set $A$. We shall write $F$ in place of $F[A]$ for the moment. Note that $F$ is not abelian if $A$ contains more than one element. Let $C$ be the commutator subgroup of $F$. Then $F / C$ is an abelian group, and it is not hard to show that $F / C$ is free abelian with basis $\{a C \mid a \in A\}$. If $a C$ is renamed $a$, we can view $F / C$ as a free abelian group with basis $A$. This indicates how a free abelian group having a given set as basis can be constructed. Every free abelian group can be constructed in this fashion, up to isomorphism. That is, if $G$ is free abelian with basis $X$, form the free group $F[X]$, form the factor group of $F[X]$ modulo its commutator subgroup, and we have a group isomorphic to $G$.

Theorems 39.7, 39.9, and 39.10 hold for free abelian groups as well as for free groups. In fact, the abelian version of Theorem 39.10 was proved for the finite rank case in Theorem 38.11. In contrast to Example 39.11 for free groups, it is true that for a free abelian group the rank of a subgroup is at most the rank of the entire group. Theorem 38.11 also showed this for the finite rank case.

In present-day expositions of algebra, a frequently used technique (particularly by the disciples of N. Bourbaki) for introducing a new algebraic entity is the following:

1. Describe algebraic properties that this algebraic entity is to possess.
2. Prove that any two algebraic entities with these properties are isomorphic, that is, that these properties characterize the entity.
3. Show that at least one such entity exists.

The next three exercises illustrate this technique for three algebraic entities, each of which we have met before. So that we do not give away their identities, we use fictitious names for them in the first two exercises. The last part of these first two exercises asks us to give the usual name for the entity.




A category $\mathcal{C}$ is a framework for talking about "things" and "structure-preserving arrows between things" in a way that is flexible enough to encompass sets and functions, vector spaces and linear maps, topological spaces and continuous maps, groups and homomorphisms, and much more. Formally, a ==category== $\mathcal{C}$ consists of two collections: a collection $\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{C})$ of objects and a collection $\mathrm{Mor}(\mathcal{C})$ of morphisms (also called arrows). Every morphism $f$ comes equipped with a source (or domain) object and a target (or codomain) object; we write $f: X \rightarrow Y$ to indicate that $f$ goes from $X$ to $Y$, with $X, Y \in \mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{C})$.

The heart of the definition is that morphisms can be composed whenever sources and targets line up, and that this composition behaves exactly like you expect from composing functions. Concretely, if $f: X \rightarrow Y$ and $g: Y \rightarrow Z$ are morphisms, then there must exist a composite morphism $g \circ f: X \rightarrow Z$. Composition is a binary operation that is only defined when the middle objects match. 


## Axioms on Categories

The first axiom is ==associativity==: whenever $f: X \rightarrow Y, g: Y \rightarrow Z$, and $h: Z \rightarrow W$ are composable, we require

$$
(h \circ g) \circ f=h \circ(g \circ f) .
$$


This guarantees that whenever three arrows can be composed, the order in which we perform the pairwise compositions does not matter; there is a single, unambiguous composite from $X$ to $W$.

The second axiom is the ==existence of identity morphisms==. For every object $X$ there is a distinguished morphism $\operatorname{id}_X: X \rightarrow X$ that behaves as a two-sided identity for composition. Precisely, if $f: X \rightarrow Y$ then

$$
f \circ \operatorname{id}_X=f, \quad \operatorname{id}_Y \circ f=f .
$$


These equations say that $\operatorname{id}_X$ does nothing to a morphism entering or leaving $X$. From these rules it already follows that the identity at each object is unique: if $e: X \rightarrow X$ satisfies the identity equations, then $e=e \circ \mathrm{id}_X=\mathrm{id}_X$.

It is often useful to organize the morphisms from $X$ to $Y$ into a set, written $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X, Y)$ or simply $\operatorname{Hom}(X, Y)$ when the category is clear from context. ==Composition== is then a family of maps

$$
\operatorname{Hom}(Y, Z) \times \operatorname{Hom}(X, Y) \longrightarrow \operatorname{Hom}(X, Z), \quad(g, f) \mapsto g \circ f
$$

that satisfy the associativity and identity laws above. A morphism $f: X \rightarrow X$ is called an ==endomorphism==; if a morphism $f: X \rightarrow Y$ admits an inverse $g: Y \rightarrow X$ with $g \circ f=\operatorname{id}_X$ and $f \circ g=\operatorname{id}_Y$, then $f$ is an ==isomorphism== and $X$ and $Y$ are essentially the same object "up to structure" inside C.



The minimality of these axioms is worth pausing over. Categories are not demanding any internal description of objects. They only ask for a network of arrows that can be composed associatively and that admits identities. This sparseness is the source of their power: the same pattern appears in countless settings. In Set, the objects are sets and the morphisms are functions; composition and identities are exactly the usual ones. In Vect $_k$, the objects are vector spaces over a field $k$ and the arrows are linear maps; the axioms hold because linear maps compose associatively and there is an identity map on every space. In Grp, objects are groups and morphisms are homomorphisms; in Top, objects are topological spaces and morphisms are continuous functions. Even a poset $(P, \leq)$ becomes a category: objects are elements of $P$, and there is a unique morphism $x \rightarrow y$ precisely when $x \leq y$; associativity amounts to transitivity, and identities correspond to reflexivity. At the other extreme, a single monoid ( $M, \cdot, 1$ ) can be viewed as a category with one object $*$ and $\operatorname{End}(*)=M$; composition is the monoid product and $\mathrm{id}_*=1$. These examples show that categories unify algebraic and order-theoretic structures under one umbrella.


Diagrams provide a convenient way to reason categorically. A string of arrows

$$
X \xrightarrow{f} Y \xrightarrow{g} Z
$$

implicitly guarantees the existence of $g \circ f: X \rightarrow Z$. A diagram is said to commute if all directed paths between the same two objects yield the same composite morphism. Commutative diagrams compress large associative/identity calculations into geometry: "going around the square equals going across the diagonal."

Small technicalities sometimes matter in set-theoretic foundations. If the class $\mathrm{Ob}(\mathcal{C})$ is actually a set, we call $\mathcal{C}$ ==small==; categories like Set or Grp are typically large because their objects form proper classes. For ordinary mathematical practice the axioms above already suffice, and one can work safely within a convenient foundational scheme.


Once the definition is internalized, many familiar properties can be rephrased in categorical terms. A morphism $m: A \rightarrow B$ is ==monic== (a categorical embedding) if $m \circ u=m \circ v$ implies $u=v$; a morphism $e: B \rightarrow C$ is ==epic== if $u \circ e=v \circ e$ implies $u=v$. Products, coproducts, kernels, and quotients become universal constructions expressed by commuting diagrams with universal properties, and functors are structure-preserving maps between categories that send objects to objects and morphisms to morphisms, preserving identities and composition.

But all of that elaboration rests on the handful of lines in the definition above. A category is a collection of objects and arrows with sources and targets; arrows compose; composition is associative; every object has an identity arrow that acts neutrally on composition. With only these ingredients, one can speak uniformly about structure across mathematics, translate results from one subject to another, and reason by diagrams rather than coordinates. That economy is the reason categories pervade modern mathematics.

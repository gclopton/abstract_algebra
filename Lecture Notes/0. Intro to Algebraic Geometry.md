

The course is an introduction to scheme theory. Schemes give a common language that genuinely links several parts of mathematics. Classical algebraic geometry over algebraically closed fields, arithmetic geometry/number theory, and even homotopy-theoretic and derived variants all sit in the same framework once you allow yourself to talk about spaces built from rings and to glue them the way topologists glue charts. With schemes you can talk about geometry over fields that are not algebraically closed, over the integers Spec $\mathbb{Z}$, and with nilpotents or multiplicities preserved rather than silently discarded. Even if one's ultimate interest is "just varieties over an algebraically closed field," the extra flexibility—tracking multiplicity, handling base change cleanly, doing intersection theory without awkward workarounds-has proven indispensable.


Fix a field $k$. One studies solution sets of polynomial equations in $n$ variables,

$$
f_1, \ldots, f_r \in k\left[x_1, \ldots, x_n\right]
$$

and looks at the subset

$$
V\left(f_1, \ldots, f_r\right)=\left\{\mathbf{x} \in k^n \mid f_i(\mathbf{x})=0, i=1, \ldots, r\right\}
$$


Over an algebraically closed field $\bar{k}$ this defines an affine algebraic variety; algebraically, the same data is the quotient ring

$$
A=k\left[x_1, \ldots, x_n\right] /\left(f_1, \ldots, f_r\right),
$$

whose spectrum Spec $A$ is the scheme-theoretic avatar of the solution set. This algebra/geometry dictionary-zeros of polynomials on one side, quotient of a polynomial ring on the other-is the bridge that scheme theory systematically generalizes.


# Example


The concrete example $C$ in the notes is the plane curve

$$
C: \quad y^2-x^3-x^2=0 \quad \text { (over } \mathbb{R} \text { in the picture). }
$$


Geometrically, the sketch marks a singular point at the origin. You can see why by differentiating the defining polynomial $F(x, y)=y^2-x^3-x^2$ : its gradient

$$
\nabla F=\left(-3 x^2-2 x, 2 y\right)
$$

vanishes at $(0,0)$, so the Jacobian has rank 0 there-hence the point is singular. Away from that point, the curve is smooth; locally each smooth point looks like a slightly bent line with a well-defined tangent. Near $(0,0)$ the equation rewrites as $y^2=x^2(x+1)$, so-over $\mathbb{R}$-two branches cross with tangents $y= \pm x$. This is exactly the sort of local phenomenon (singularities, multiplicities, behavior under base change from $\mathbb{R}$ to $\mathbb{C}$, or to finite fields) that schemes are designed to record faithfully and to manipulate functorially.


---


Classically, we study solution sets of polynomial equations and distinguish smooth from singular points via derivatives. Schemes replace bare solution sets with spaces built from commutative rings and glued from affine charts, giving a framework that unifies geometry across bases, encodes arithmetic, and treats singularities and nilpotents honestly rather than ignoring them. That is why schemes matter—and why we start with varieties, then move quickly beyond them.

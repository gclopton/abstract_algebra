
$f: A \rightarrow B$ is both injective and surjective; equivalently, there exists an inverse function $f^{-1}: B \rightarrow A$ with $f^{-1}(f(a))=a$ and $f\left(f^{-1}(b)\right)=b$.


# Examples


**A shift of the reals:**
$h: \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}, h(x)=x+3$. Different inputs give different outputs, and for any $y$ there's $x=y-3$ with $h(x)=y$. The inverse is $h^{-1}(y)=y-3$.

**The exponential with its natural target:**
$h: \mathbb{R} \rightarrow(0, \infty), h(x)=e^x$. Strictly increasing (hence injective), and every positive real is $e^{\ln y}$ (surjective). Inverse $h^{-1}(y)=\ln y$.

**A permutation of a finite set:**
$h:\{1,2,3\} \rightarrow\{1,2,3\}$ with $h(1)=2, h(2)=3, h(3)=1$. Each element is hit exactly once; the inverse sends $2 \mapsto 1,3 \mapsto 2,1 \mapsto 3$.
